# Letter to the editor – Hong Kong's problem with light pollution

Dear Editor,

I am writing in response to the article "Hong Kong's problem with light pollution", published in yesterday's Hong Kong Times. As someone who has lived in Mong Kok all my life, I was not surprised to read that our city has one of the worst levels of light pollution in the world, but I was alarmed to learn how far its effects reach. I strongly agree with the writer that the problem has been ignored for too long, and I would like to share my views on why it matters and what should be done.

Many Hong Kong people treat our neon-lit skyline as a symbol of success. However, the article reminds us that the glow comes at a price. Sea turtles and other marine animals that depend on moonlight to navigate are confused by the brightness along our coasts, and migratory birds passing through the city every autumn are drawn off course by illuminated towers, often crashing into glass windows. These creatures cannot write to a newspaper, so it is our responsibility to speak for them.

The effects on people are just as serious. I live opposite a shopping mall whose advertising screen flashes until well after midnight. Even with the curtains drawn, my bedroom is never properly dark, and I often struggle to fall asleep. Doctors have warned that constant exposure to artificial light at night disturbs our body clocks and has been linked to insomnia, obesity and depression. In a city where students and workers are already exhausted, we can hardly afford another threat to our sleep.

What frustrates me most is that this problem is entirely preventable. At present, the government relies on a voluntary charter that asks businesses to switch off decorative lighting at eleven o'clock at night. Unfortunately, a request without consequences is easily ignored, and a walk through Tsim Sha Tsui at one in the morning proves how little it has achieved. I therefore urge the government to introduce legislation, as cities such as Paris and Seoul have done, setting clear switch-off hours and brightness limits for outdoor signs, with fines for those who break the rules.

Individuals and businesses also have a part to play. Shops could install shielded lights that point downwards rather than into the sky, and residents could support the cause by turning off unnecessary balcony and garden lights. Schools could teach students about the issue, since young people have proved to be powerful advocates for environmental change.

Hong Kong is rightly proud of its glittering skyline, but a city that never sleeps should not become a city where nobody can sleep. I hope the article will encourage readers, businesses and officials to act before the damage to our wildlife and our health becomes irreversible.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong
