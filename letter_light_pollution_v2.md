# Letter to the editor – Hong Kong's problem with light pollution (version 2)

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest, and some sadness, your article "Hong Kong's problem with light pollution" published yesterday. Last month my school's science club visited the Astropark in Sai Kung, and even there, far from the city centre, the orange glow on the horizon drowned out all but the brightest stars. It was a vivid lesson in how far our light now spreads. The article confirmed what we saw, and I would like to add my voice to the call for action.

Your writer rightly pointed out that the first victims are animals that cannot complain. The corals of Hoi Ha Wan release their eggs by the light of the moon, and horseshoe crabs come ashore to breed guided by its glow. When our coastline shines all night, these ancient rhythms are thrown into chaos. Further north, the black-faced spoonbills and thousands of other migratory birds that rest at Mai Po each winter navigate by the stars. Floodlit towers and glowing billboards lead them astray and exhaust them before they reach their destination.

Humans are not spared either. My younger brother sleeps beside a window facing a brightly lit car park, and he wakes tired almost every morning. Scientists have shown that artificial light at night suppresses melatonin, the hormone that tells our bodies to rest, and it has been linked to poor concentration, obesity and even certain cancers. For a city that prides itself on productivity, this is a cost we should take seriously.

Some readers may argue that bright lights keep streets safe and give Hong Kong the dazzling image that attracts tourists. I understand this concern, but the two aims are not in conflict. Street lamps and security lighting can stay on, while decorative floodlights and giant advertising screens, which serve no safety purpose, can be dimmed or switched off after ten o'clock. Visitors come for our skyline at dusk, not at three in the morning when the shops are closed and only the birds and the sleepless are watching.

I therefore propose three practical steps. The government should replace the current voluntary charter with enforceable rules on the brightness, direction and operating hours of outdoor lighting. Building owners should fit timers, motion sensors and shielded, warm-coloured LED fixtures, which would also cut their electricity bills. Finally, our country parks should be protected as dark-sky areas, so that future generations can still look up and see the Milky Way from within our own city.

Hong Kong has shown before that it can change its habits quickly once the public understands why. I hope your article is the beginning of a serious conversation, and that the next time my club visits Sai Kung, we will count more stars than streetlights.

Yours faithfully,
Karen Lee
