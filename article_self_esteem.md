# Article for the school magazine – Student self-esteem survey (about 400 words)

Are We Really That Hard on Ourselves?

Last month, a youth magazine published the results of a survey of 4,000 students from 25 Hong Kong schools. It measured self-esteem in four areas: how students rate their intellectual ability, their happiness, their anxiety and their popularity. The conclusion was blunt. Hong Kong students score below international norms in every area. When I first read this, I was ready to dismiss it. After a week of watching my classmates more closely, I am not so sure.

On intellectual ability, the survey matches what I see every day. Our school is full of capable people, yet almost nobody admits it. Ask a top student how she did in a test and she will say "badly" before the papers are even returned. In a culture where a mark of 85 brings the question "what happened to the other 15?", it is hardly surprising that we learn to think of ourselves as never quite good enough.

On anxiety, the survey is, if anything, too kind. Walk past the library at lunchtime during examination season and you will see students eating with one hand and highlighting notes with the other. Several of my friends sleep less than six hours a night, and one has started seeing the school social worker about panic attacks. I suspect many students did not report their true anxiety levels because they consider them normal.

Where I disagree is on happiness and popularity. The survey suggests we are lonely and unhappy, but that is not the school I know. Our classrooms are noisy with laughter, our sports teams are packed, and the group chats never fall silent. The difference, I think, is how the questions were asked. If you ask a Hong Kong teenager whether he is "popular", he will say no because the word sounds boastful. Ask whether he has friends he can rely on, and the answer is almost always yes. Modesty, not misery, may explain part of the gap.

So what should we take from the survey? It is right that we are too harsh on our own abilities and too used to living with stress. It is wrong, or at least incomplete, about our social lives. The real lesson is that we need to change the way we talk about success. The next time a friend scores 85, try saying "well done" instead of asking about the rest. Self-esteem, after all, is built one conversation at a time.

By Chris Wong, 5B
