# Model essay – Should community service be compulsory in secondary schools? (about 400 words)

Task: Your school magazine is running a series on school policy. Write an article giving your views on whether community service should be compulsory for all secondary students.

Compulsory Community Service: A Burden or a Blessing?

Every year, thousands of Hong Kong students spend their weekends serving meals to the elderly, cleaning beaches or tutoring children in low-income families. Some do it willingly, while others do it only because their school requires it. This raises a question that has divided our staff room and our classrooms alike: should community service be compulsory for every secondary student? Having completed sixty hours of service myself, I believe it should, for three reasons.

First, compulsory service introduces students to experiences they would never choose on their own. When I was assigned to visit elderly residents in Sham Shui Po last year, I complained for a week beforehand. Yet by the end of the first afternoon, listening to a ninety-year-old man describe the Hong Kong of the 1950s, I had learned more about our city than in any history lesson. Many of my classmates tell similar stories. Left to ourselves, most teenagers would stay in our comfort zones, and the chance to grow would be lost.

Second, service builds skills that examinations cannot measure. Organising a charity sale teaches planning and teamwork. Explaining fractions to a nine-year-old demands patience and clear communication. Universities and employers increasingly look for these qualities, and a certificate of service hours is proof that a student has developed them. In this sense, compulsory service is not a distraction from our studies but a valuable part of our education.

Critics argue that forcing students to volunteer destroys the spirit of volunteering. If the service is unwilling, they say, it is not really service at all. I understand this concern, but the evidence suggests otherwise. Research in both Hong Kong and overseas shows that students who are required to serve at school are more likely to continue volunteering as adults. A habit that begins as an obligation often ends as a genuine commitment.

Of course, the policy must be well designed. Students should be allowed to choose the type of service that interests them, and the hours required should be reasonable so that studies do not suffer. With these safeguards in place, the benefits far outweigh the costs.

Community service may begin as a burden, but for most students it becomes a blessing. Our school should keep the requirement, and others should follow.

By Chris Wong, 5B
