## Question 2 – Short story: "It had truly been an unforgettable summer."

"Paddles up!" Coach Lam's voice rang across the bay as twenty of us lifted our paddles together. Six weeks earlier, I would never have believed I could be sitting in a dragon boat at six in the morning, let alone enjoying it.

My name is Hayley, and this summer my parents sent me to Lantau Island Adventure Camp against my will. I was fifteen, painfully shy and happiest in my bedroom with my phone. The thought of sharing a tent with strangers and sweating under the sun filled me with dread. On the first day, I hid at the back of the group and counted the hours until I could go home.

Everything changed when Coach Lam announced that the camp would compete in the Lantau Youth Dragon Boat Race at the end of the summer. "Nobody sits out," he said, looking straight at me. "A boat only moves when every paddle moves together."

The first week was torture. My arms ached, my palms blistered, and I was always half a beat behind everyone else. Our boat kept drifting sideways because of my weak strokes. Some of the older campers sighed loudly whenever I made a mistake, and one evening I cried quietly behind the boathouse, ready to call my mum and beg her to collect me.

That was where Mei found me. She was the drummer of our boat, a tiny girl with an enormous voice, and instead of telling me to toughen up, she simply sat down beside me. "I was the worst paddler last year," she said. "Now I set the rhythm for everyone. Come on, I'll practise with you."

Every morning after that, Mei and I went down to the water before breakfast. She taught me to twist my body, to reach forward and pull the water past me instead of slapping at it. Slowly, my strokes grew longer and stronger. Slowly, the others stopped sighing and started cheering. By the fourth week, I had moved to the front row, where the strongest paddlers set the pace.

Race day arrived with a cloudless sky. When the horn sounded, Mei's drum thundered and our paddles struck the water as one. I could not hear the crowd or the other boats, only the beat and my own breathing. We crossed the line in second place, a few seconds behind the champions, but it felt like gold.

Standing on the pier afterwards, my paddle over my shoulder and my backpack ready for the ferry home, I looked back at the boats resting on the water. I had arrived as a girl who wanted to hide. I was leaving with blistered hands, new friends and a confidence I had never known. It had truly been an unforgettable summer.

## Question 3 – Essay: Why trendy foods and drinks pop up so quickly nowadays

Ten years ago, bubble tea was something you bought from a small shop near school. Today, a new food craze seems to appear every few months, from matcha lattes and Korean corn dogs to the Dubai chocolate cookies that recently sold out across Hong Kong. Why do these trends spread so fast nowadays? In my view, social media, the hunger for novelty, clever marketing and globalisation all play a part.

The most obvious reason is social media. Platforms such as Instagram, TikTok and Xiaohongshu have turned eating into a visual activity. A colourful drink or a cookie oozing with pistachio filling is photographed, filmed and shared with millions of viewers within hours. When a popular influencer posts a video of a new snack, thousands of followers rush to try it so that they can post their own version. Food that looks good on camera therefore spreads far more quickly than it did when people only discovered new dishes by word of mouth.

A second reason is that people today, especially the young, crave novelty. Life in a big city can feel repetitive, and trying a new food is a cheap and easy way to feel excited. Queueing for an hour outside a new dessert shop becomes an experience in itself, something to talk about with friends and classmates. Many teenagers also fear missing out. If everyone at school is drinking a new flavour of bubble tea, nobody wants to be the only one who has not tasted it.

Businesses are quick to take advantage of this. Modern food companies can design, test and launch a new product within weeks rather than years. They deliberately create limited editions and seasonal flavours so that customers feel they must buy now or lose the chance forever. Chains also copy one another at great speed. As soon as one café introduces a successful item, its competitors release similar versions, and the trend multiplies across the city before it has even reached its peak.

Finally, globalisation has opened the world's kitchens to everyone. Ingredients from Japan, Korea or the Middle East can be imported easily, and online videos teach small shop owners how to make foreign dishes within a day. A dessert that becomes popular in Dubai can be on sale in Mong Kok a month later.

In conclusion, trendy foods and drinks appear so quickly because social media spreads images instantly, consumers hunger for new experiences, businesses race to profit from every craze and global ingredients are only a click away. These trends may fade as fast as they arrive, but as long as people enjoy sharing what they eat, the next big flavour is never far away.

## Question 4 – Blog post: My month as an influencer

My Month as an Influencer: Why I Am Logging Off

Exactly thirty days ago, I posted my first video as a "lifestyle influencer". Today I am writing the final post of my experiment, and I want to be honest about what the month was really like, because it was nothing like the glamorous life on your screen.

It started as a dare. My friends joked that I filmed my food and outfits anyway, so why not turn it into something? I set myself a simple goal: post every single day for a month, grow an audience and see whether I could make it as a creator. I bought a ring light, planned my content and told myself it would be fun.

The first week actually was fun. I filmed café reviews, a tour of my messy desk and a "study with me" video that somehow reached twenty thousand views. Strangers left kind comments, and watching my follower count climb gave me a buzz every time I unlocked my phone. For the first time, I understood why so many people want this job.

Then reality set in. Posting daily meant that every moment of my life became potential content. I could not eat lunch without thinking about the lighting. I spent three or four hours every evening filming, editing and replying to comments, and my homework piled up. Worse, the algorithm is cruel. One video would get thousands of views, and the next, which took twice as long to make, would get two hundred. I began checking the numbers every ten minutes, and my mood rose and fell with them.

The comments changed too. Alongside the kind messages came strangers criticising my voice, my room and my face. I knew I should ignore them, but at midnight, alone with my phone, that was easier said than done. By week three, I was sleeping five hours a night, snapping at my family and feeling anxious whenever I had not posted.

So here is the truth about continuing. Being an influencer is not a hobby. It is a full-time job with no days off, no salary at the start, and a boss, the algorithm, that never explains its decisions. To keep going, I would have to sacrifice my studies, my sleep and much of my privacy. I have decided that the price is too high for me right now.

I am not deleting my account. I will still share things I genuinely love, when I feel like it, without chasing numbers. To anyone dreaming of becoming a creator, I would say try it, but set boundaries first, and remember that the people you admire online show you the highlight reel, not the exhausting hours behind it.

Thanks for following along. See you offline.

## Question 5 – Advice column reply to N. L. Wong

Dear N. L. Wong,

Thank you for writing to us, and for being so honest about a situation that many of our readers will recognise. Hearing your parents discuss your grades and personal life in front of relatives is embarrassing and hurtful, and your wish for privacy is completely reasonable. The good news is that this problem can be solved, but it will take some courage and careful planning on your part.

First, try to understand why your parents do this. In many families, talking about children is simply the way adults make conversation at gatherings. Your parents may be comparing notes with other parents, seeking reassurance or even hoping a relative will offer useful advice. This does not make it right, but seeing it as a habit rather than an attack will help you approach them calmly instead of angrily.

Second, choose the right moment to speak to them. Do not raise the issue in the middle of a family dinner or straight after an incident, when emotions are running high. Instead, pick a quiet evening at home, perhaps while helping with the dishes, and ask whether you can talk about something that has been bothering you. Starting with "I feel" rather than "You always" makes a huge difference. You might say, "I feel embarrassed when my grades are discussed in front of Auntie and Uncle, and I would really appreciate it if we could keep those things within our family."

Third, show them that you are taking responsibility. Since you admit that you are struggling at school, tell your parents what you plan to do about it, whether that means asking a teacher for extra help, joining a study group or setting a revision timetable. Parents often gossip because they are worried. If they can see that you are facing the problem seriously, they will have less reason to seek opinions from relatives and more reason to trust you.

If you are afraid they will not listen, put your thoughts in a letter first. Writing lets you explain your feelings without being interrupted, and it gives your parents time to think before they respond. You could also ask a trusted adult, such as an older cousin, a teacher or a school social worker, to help you open the conversation.

Finally, be patient. Your parents have been doing this for years, and they may slip up once or twice even after agreeing to change. A gentle reminder afterwards, in private, will work far better than a public outburst.

You have already taken the hardest step by recognising the problem and asking for help. With a calm conversation and a clear plan, I am confident your parents will begin to understand you better.

Wishing you all the best,

The Youths Advice Columnist
