# Part B – Questions 6 to 8 (about 450 words each)

## Question 6 – Debate speech: Street smarts are not more important than book smarts

Good afternoon, adjudicators, teachers and fellow students.

The motion before us today claims that street smarts are more important than book smarts. As captain of the opposition, I will argue that this is simply not true. Street smarts may be useful, but they are not more important than book smarts, and I will give you three reasons why.

My first reason is that book smarts are the foundation on which street smarts are built. Our opponents will tell you stories of clever people who can talk their way out of trouble or spot a bargain in a market. But where did that cleverness come from? A doctor's instinct in an emergency comes from years of studying anatomy. A businessman's feel for a good deal comes from understanding accounts and contracts. Even reading a map or a bus timetable requires skills learned in a classroom. Without book knowledge, so-called street smarts are just guesswork, and guesswork fails when the situation is new.

My second reason is that book smarts open doors that street smarts cannot. In Hong Kong, as in every modern society, qualifications matter. A person cannot become an engineer, a nurse, a lawyer or a pilot by being charming and quick-witted. These careers require examinations, licences and years of structured learning. Book smarts are also transferable. A student who understands physics in Hong Kong can apply it in Toronto or Tokyo, whereas street smarts gained in Mong Kok may be useless in a Berlin suburb. Knowledge travels; local cunning does not.

My third reason is that in today's world, book smarts protect us from being fooled. We live surrounded by fake news, online scams and advertisements that twist statistics. Street-smart people pride themselves on not being cheated, but the scams of the twenty-first century are not run by pickpockets. They are run by people who exploit our ignorance of science, finance and history. The only defence is genuine knowledge. The person who understands how interest rates work will not fall for a get-rich-quick scheme, no matter how confident the salesman sounds.

Let me be clear. We are not saying that street smarts are worthless. Common sense and the ability to read people are valuable. But valuable is not the same as more important. A ship needs both a captain with experience and a chart drawn by scientists, and nobody would throw away the chart.

Book smarts build the foundation, open the doors and protect us from deception. For these three reasons, we firmly oppose the motion that street smarts are more important than book smarts.

Thank you.

## Question 7 – Letter to the editor: Raising the legal drinking age to 21

Dear Editor,

I am writing in response to the recent article in the Hong Kong Daily proposing that the legal drinking age in Hong Kong should be raised from 18 to 21. Although some readers may see this as an attack on personal freedom, I fully support the idea, and I would like to explain my position with three reasons.

First, there is strong scientific evidence that the human brain continues to develop until around the age of 25. The part of the brain responsible for judgement and self-control is the last to mature, which is exactly why teenagers are more likely to take risks. Alcohol consumed during these years can cause lasting damage to memory and learning, and young people who start drinking early are far more likely to develop a dependence later in life. Delaying legal access by three years would give the brain valuable time to mature before it is exposed to alcohol.

Second, raising the age would protect students at the most vulnerable stage of their lives. At 18, most Hong Kong young people are entering university, where drinking games and late-night parties quickly become part of the culture. Every year, newspapers report cases of students being hospitalised after binge drinking at orientation camps. A higher legal age would not stop every incident, but it would make it harder for first-year students to buy alcohol, and it would send a clear message that heavy drinking is not a normal part of growing up.

Third, the experience of other places shows that such laws work. When the United States raised its drinking age to 21, road accidents involving young drivers fell significantly, and studies found that the overall level of drinking among teenagers also dropped. Hong Kong already restricts smoking and gambling for the protection of young people, so applying the same logic to alcohol is simply consistent.

Critics argue that 18-year-olds can vote and marry, so they should be trusted to drink. However, the law already recognises that different activities carry different risks. Nobody suggests that an 18-year-old should be allowed to drive a bus or buy a gun. Others say the law would be impossible to enforce, but the same was said about the smoking ban in restaurants, which is now widely accepted.

Raising the drinking age to 21 would protect young brains, reduce dangerous drinking on campuses and follow the proven example of other countries. I urge the government to consider the proposal seriously.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong

## Question 8 – Email to Ms Wong: Suggesting a local athlete for the seminar

To: Ms Wong
From: Chris Wong, Chairperson, Sports Club
Subject: Suggested speaker for the Sports Club seminar

Dear Ms Wong,

Thank you for asking the Sports Club committee to suggest a local athlete for our seminar next month. After discussing several possibilities, we would like to recommend Siobhan Haughey, the Olympic medallist swimmer, and I would like to explain our reasons and what we hope she could cover.

We have chosen Siobhan Haughey for three main reasons. First, she is the most successful swimmer Hong Kong has ever produced, winning Olympic medals at both the Tokyo and Paris Games. Students who have watched her races on television will be excited to hear from her in person, which should guarantee a full hall. Second, her story is especially relevant to our students. She grew up in Hong Kong, trained in local pools before dawn and still managed to complete a university degree in the United States. She is living proof that a Hong Kong student can balance study and sport at the highest level. Third, she is known for being humble and articulate in interviews, so we are confident she would connect well with a young audience and answer questions patiently.

As for the content of the seminar, we suggest that it be divided into three parts. In the first part, Ms Haughey could describe her journey from a Hong Kong primary school swimmer to the Olympic podium, including the setbacks along the way, such as the injuries and the disappointing results she faced before her breakthrough. Students often believe that champions never fail, and hearing otherwise would be inspiring.

In the second part, she could share practical advice on time management and mental preparation. Many of our members struggle to fit training around homework and examinations, and they would benefit from learning how she organised her days as a student-athlete and how she copes with pressure before a major race.

In the final part, we propose a question-and-answer session, followed by a short photo opportunity. If possible, we could also invite members of the swimming team to receive a few technique tips from her at the school pool.

We believe a seminar of about ninety minutes in the school hall would be ideal, and we would be happy to help with promotion, seating and the preparation of a souvenir. If you agree with our suggestion, we could contact the Hong Kong Sports Institute to make a formal invitation.

Thank you for considering our proposal. I look forward to hearing your views.

Best regards,
Chris Wong
Chairperson, Sports Club
